Naive-Bayes Classification of Text Documents

In this assignment, a simple Naive-Bayes model was created to classify review text documents as positive or negative. A given dataset contains many reviews, all labeled, and is used to both train and test the model.

First, to create the model, we iterate through all the test documents to determine the likelhoods and priors. The following formulas were implemented in Python as the function train_nb(documents, labels):

Prior

The log probability of each class \(c_j\) occurring:

\[ \log P(c_j) = \log \frac{N_{c_j}}{N} \]

where \(N_{c_j}\) is the number of training documents labeled \(c_j\) and \(N\) is the total number of training documents.

Likelihood

The Laplace-smoothed log probability of each word \(w_k\) given class \(c_j\):

\[ \log P(w_k \mid c_j) = \log \frac{n_{k,j} + 1}{n_j + |V|} \]

where \(n_{k,j}\) is the number of times \(w_k\) occurs in documents of class \(c_j\), \(n_j\) is the total number of words in documents of class \(c_j\), and \(|V|\) is the size of the vocabulary.

Following the Naive-Bayes implementation, another function, def classify_nb(document, P_c, P_w_c), was created to calculate the posterior of a document belonging to a given class:

Posterior
\[ P(c_j \mid d) \propto P(c_j) \prod_{k=1}^{|d|} P(w_k \mid c_j) \]

In log space, the product becomes a sum:

\[ \log P(c_j \mid d) = \log P(c_j) + \sum_{k=1}^{|d|} \log P(w_k \mid c_j) \]

where \(d\) is the document and \(w_k\) is the \(k\)-th word in \(d\).

Error Analysis

An 81.54% accuracy is okay for what this model is. However, Naive-Bayes has pitfalls. All it does is count the number of words in a document to establish probabilities. No significance is given to the order of words, or the meanings of sentences.

For examples, one of the documents it misclassified was the following:

"i agree with other reviewers that it feels good and does n't smell too much , however , i 've experimented with it several times to confirm my findings , and it turns out to give me really bad blackheads . i 'm 25 with an oily t-zone and very dry facial skin . on mornings after using this cream , i have nasty blackheads on my forehead and chin . there are better products out there"

The model classified it as positive, while it's actually negative. The log score for positive is -517.2, and the negative score is -522.3, which was a close comparison. It's easy to see why it got confused: The document contains positive phrases like "I agree", "feels good" and "better", as well as negative ones like "really bad" and "nasty". For a model that just compares word occurence, it makes sense that the misclassification happened with close probabilities. We know the review is negative because we understand the meaning of the person's experience with the product.

Another examples is this one:

"the video editing capability is tough to deal with and not impressive when you figure it out . the tools are not explained and there is n't as much to do with it as they imply . may be nice for birthday party editing , i do n't know . i did n't like it . it 's not worth $100. the other capabilities might be nice , but from what i 've dealt with , that 's not the case"

The negative score was -495.3 and the positive score was -493.5. Again, very close, but it was classified as positive even though it's negative. With a combination of positive words like "impressive" and "nice", and negative ones like "not", the confusion makes sense.

Finally, here's a case that was strongly misclassified as negative for an interesting reason:

"maybe it 's because i was upgrading from an older version - - 2002 - - but i found the new features to be very good . for example.. . the junk mail feature works better than any other i have used . it was catching junk from the very beginning , and has missed very few things . i have only had one thing put in the junk folder that was not junk email . also the security features are far superior to the 2002 version . and the calendar is more flexible and easier to use . installation was seemless . i think it might be a little overpriced , but considering how long i will keep it , it 's not too bad"

The word "junk" was used many times, which in the context of reviews is usually negative, but in this case, it was actually just referring to junk email. And even though the review is mostly positive, there are some listed cons using words like "overpriced" and "bad". So even with positive words in the review, like "good", "superior" and "easier", it wasn't enough to sway the model with the many occurances of "junk". Another interesting point is that normally, the word "seamless" would be considered positive, but it was misspelled in this review, so the model likely didn't count it as positive, if it even counted it at all. This reveals another weakness of Naive-Bayes - there's little tolerance for misspellings - typos can't be grouped as the same word because the model can't tell. The positive log score was -803.1, while the negative score was -792.2 - significantly lower. So the review was misclassified as negative, and the model was confident about it.

Overall, the Naive-Bayes classifier usually gets the answer right, but when it doesn't, it's usually because it lacks context as to why the words were used the way they were. It cannot understand meaning, which is often important when analyzing text, even if it's just for broad classifications.
