Notes

  • For the training data RMSEs, the RMSE does increase over the first 3 polynomial degrees, however, starting at degree 4, the RMSEs's increase by 2 orders of magnitude with a significant jump at degree 6.
  • For the test data RMSEs, it's clear that they increase exponentially with polynomial degree, especially considering that the log scale graph is largely linear.
  • The reason the RMSE gets worse as the degree increases is likely due to poor system conditioning and the different scales of each feature in the dataset, ultimately resulting in overfitting and floating point error.
  • The Phi matrix constructed from the data points and polynomial degree has the shape \((K \cdot L + 1) \times N\), where \(K\) is the polynomial degree, \(L\) is the number of dimensions, the 1 is the bias, and \(N\) is the number of data points. When calculating the pseudo-inverse, \(\Phi \Phi^T \) then has shape \((K \cdot L + 1) \times (K \cdot L + 1) \). For this matrix to have an inverse, \((K \cdot L + 1) \leq N\), otherwise it is not full rank. And the closer it is to N, the more singular it becomes - overfitting.
  • Degrees 1-3 follow the expected trend of decreasing RMSE. This makes sense because for degree 3, \(\Phi \Phi^T \) has shape \(40 \times 40 \), which is reasonable comapared to \(N\). Beyond that, the higher polynomials result in very high and very small numbers in \(\Phi\). For example, feature 12 is usually close to 400, while feature 1 is usually between 0 and 1. Raise that to the 7th power, and you get a huge range of values, that even with float64 introduce inaccuracies through the matrix multiplications. That's why the errors explode.
  • This applies to the test data as well, but it's worse because the model also suffers from overfitting especially at higher polynomials.
  • Past degree 3, the results simply aren't reliable, hence the inexplicable spike at degree 6 in the training data, and the explosive growth in error for the test data.

Notes

  • The degree 2 polynomial generally follows the training data well. There are some inaccuracies, but it captures the general trend. Though not shown, degrees 1 and 3 behave similarly.
  • At degree 4, some overfitting starts to occur. Especially since there's no training data between \(x = 30 \) and \(x = 70, \) the curve bulges upward slightly. It so happens that this bulge loosely follows the test data, but this is never guaranteed.
  • For degree 7, significant overfitting is clearly visible.   • Overall, comparing the distribution of training and test data, it's clear that there's bias in the training set, which does translate to the model. This is visible in all the plots, as the curves tend to miss some of the trends of the test data.

d) L2-Regularized Polynomial Degree 8 Regression for the 2nd Dataset Feature

A new Pyython function - L2_1d_regularized_regression() - was created to implement L2-regularized (Ridge) regression using the first 100 data points in the dataset, but only for the 2nd feature. The regression was performed with a degree 8 polynomial and each of the following values of \(\lambda \): {0; 0:01; 0:1; 1; 10; 100; 1000}. Finally, 10-fold cross-validation was used to decide on the best value of \(\lambda \).

The result is plotted as average validation set error versus the regularizing constant. The unregularized result, \(\lambda = 0 \) appears as a red dotted horizontal line as a baseline.
