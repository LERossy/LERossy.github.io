Assignment 1

Part 1: Shortest Distance Between a Point and a Function

Find the distance between any point P and \(y = x^2 + 5\) → Parabola shifted up by 5

In other words, find the point on the curve where a line segment connecting P and the curve is perpendicular to the curve.

There are multiple ways to solve this problem for parabolas and any kind of function, both Numerical and Analytical. These solution methods, as well as coded equivalents for numerical algorithms are presented below.

Analytical Approach

Distance function:

\[ d = \sqrt{(x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2} \]

To avoid dealing with the square root, knowing both functions have the same minimium, minimize the distance squared instead:

\[ D(x) = (x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 \]

Sub in the parabola:

\[ D(x) = (x - x_0)^2 + (x^2 + 5 - y_0)^2 \]

Differentiate \(D(x)\) to optimize:

\[ \begin{aligned} D'(x) &= 2(x - x_0) + 2(x^2 + 5 - y_0)(2x) \\ D'(x) &= 2x - 2x_0 + 4x^3 + 20x - 4xy_0 \\ D'(x) &= 2\left(2x^3 + (11 - 2y_0)x - x_0\right) \end{aligned} \]

Set derivative to 0 to find minimum:

\[ 0 = 2x^3 + (11 - 2y_0)x - x_0 \]

Logically, we know any solutions to this equation will be a minimum, because the maximum distance between a point and any function that goes to infinity, like a parabola, will be infinite.

Similarly, because we know \(D(x)\) physically represents the squared distance to a function, there will have to be at least 1 real solution.

Solve for \(x\) for the following points: \([(0,0);\ (-4,0);\ (-8,0);\ (2,0);\ (6,0)]\)

1. \((0, 0)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= 2x^3 + 11x \\ 0 &= x\underbrace{(2x^2 + 11)}_{\text{complex roots}} \end{aligned} \] \[ \therefore\ x = 0 \] \[ \begin{aligned} D(0) &= (0 - 0)^2 + (0^2 + 5 - 0)^2 \\ D(0) &= 25 \\ d(0,0) &= \sqrt{D(0)} \\ d &= 5 \end{aligned} \]

Numerical Approach

Algorithm A: Newton-Raphson

Newton-Raphson requires the second derivative of \(D(x)\), which is determined below:

\[ \begin{aligned} y &= x^2 + 5 \\ D(x) &= (x - x_0)^2 + (x^2 + 5 - y_0)^2 \\ D'(x) &= 4x^3 + (22 - 4y_0)x - 2x_0 \quad (\text{from above}) \\ D''(x) &= 12x^2 + 22 - 4y_0 \end{aligned} \]

For each iteration, to find the next guess, the first derivative divided by the second derivative of \(D(x)\) is subtracted from the previous guess. This is to find the root of \(D'(x)\), which is where the minimum of \(D(x)\) occurs.

For the analytical approach, the distances were determined to 4 decimal places, so the algorithm will be applied to that accuracy by hand. Finer accuracy can be achieved with a computer.

Starting guess: \(x = 0\)

Find distance for \([(0,0);\ (-4,0);\ (-8,0);\ (2,0);\ (6,0)]\)

1. \((0, 0)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= x - \frac{D'(x)}{D''(x)} \\ x_1 &= 0 - \frac{4(0)^3 + (22 - 4(0))(0) - 2(0)}{12(0)^2 + 22 - 4(0)} \\ x_1 &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

No change.

Now calculate d:

\[ \begin{aligned} D(0) &= (0 - 0)^2 + (0^2 + 5 - 0)^2 = 25 \\ d(0,0) &= \sqrt{D(0)} \\ d(0,0) &= 5 \end{aligned} \]
2. \((-4, 0)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 - \frac{4(0)^3 + (22 - 4(0))(0) - (2(-4))}{12(0)^2 + 22 - 4(0)} \\ x_1 &= -\frac{4}{11} = -0.36363 \\[1ex] x_2 &= -\frac{4}{11} - \frac{4\left(-\frac{4}{11}\right)^3 + (22 - 4(0))\left(-\frac{4}{11}\right) + 8}{12\left(-\frac{4}{11}\right)^2 + 22 - 4(0)} \\ x_2 &= -0.35548 \\[1ex] x_3 &= -0.35548 - \frac{4(-0.35548)^3 + 22(-0.35548) + 8}{12(-0.35548)^2 + 22} \\ x_3 &= -0.35547 \\ x_3 &\approx -0.3555 \end{aligned} \]

From above:

\[ \begin{aligned} D(-0.3555) &= 39.5622 \\ d(-4,0) &= 6.2898 \end{aligned} \]
3. \((-8, 0)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} D'(x) &= 4x^3 + (22 - 4(0))x - 2(-8) \\ D'(x) &= 4x^3 + 22x + 16 \\ D''(x) &= 12x^2 + 22 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 - \frac{4(0)^3 + 22(0) + 16}{12(0)^2 + 22} \\ x_1 &= -\frac{8}{11} = -0.72727 \\[1ex] x_2 &= -\frac{8}{11} - \frac{4\left(-\frac{8}{11}\right)^3 + 22\left(-\frac{8}{11}\right) + 16}{12\left(-\frac{8}{11}\right)^2 + 22} \\ x_2 &= -\frac{8}{11} + 0.05428 \\ x_2 &= -0.67299 \\[1ex] x_3 &= -0.67299 - \frac{4(-0.67299)^3 + 22(-0.67299) + 16}{12(-0.67299)^2 + 22} \\ &= -0.67299 + 0.0009 \\ x_3 &= -0.67208 \\[1ex] x_4 &= -0.67208 - \frac{4(-0.67208)^3 + 22(-0.67208) + 16}{12(-0.67208)^2 + 22} \\ &= -0.67208 + 0.000003 \\ x_4 &\approx -0.6721 \end{aligned} \]

From above:

\[ \begin{aligned} D(-0.6721) &= 83.4194 \\ d(-8,0) &= 9.1334 \end{aligned} \]
4. \((2, 0)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} D'(x) &= 4x^3 + 22x - 4 \\ D''(x) &= 12x^2 + 22 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 - \frac{4(0)^3 + 22(0) - 4}{12(0)^2 + 22} \\ x_1 &= \frac{2}{11} = 0.18182 \\[1ex] x_2 &= \frac{2}{11} - \frac{4\left(\frac{2}{11}\right)^3 + 22\left(\frac{2}{11}\right) - 4}{12\left(\frac{2}{11}\right)^2 + 22} \\ x_2 &= 0.18182 - 0.00107 \\ x_2 &= 0.18074 \\[1ex] x_3 &= 0.18074 - \frac{4(0.18074)^3 + 22(0.18074) - 4}{12(0.18074)^2 + 22} \\ &= 0.18074 + 0.0000046 \\ x_3 &\approx 0.1807 \end{aligned} \]

From above:

\[ \begin{aligned} D(0.1807) &= 28.6374 \\ d(2,0) &= 5.3514 \end{aligned} \]
5. \((6, 0)\)
\[ \begin{aligned} D'(x) &= 4x^3 + 22x - 12 \\ D''(x) &= 12x^2 + 22 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0 - \frac{4(0)^3 + 22(0) - 12}{12(0)^2 + 22} \\ x_1 &= \frac{6}{11} = 0.54545 \\[1ex] x_2 &= \frac{6}{11} - \frac{4\left(\frac{6}{11}\right)^3 + 22\left(\frac{6}{11}\right) - 12}{12\left(\frac{6}{11}\right)^2 + 22} \\ &= 0.54545 - 0.02539 \\ x_2 &= 0.52007 \\[1ex] x_3 &= 0.52007 - \frac{4(0.52007)^3 + 22(0.52007) - 12}{12(0.52007)^2 + 22} \\ &= 0.52007 - 0.00017 \\ x_3 &= 0.51990 \\ x_3 &\approx 0.5199 \end{aligned} \]

From above:

Algorithm B: Golden Section Search

This algorithm works by repeatedly narrowing down a section [a,b], where the solution is known to be, by a factor of phi until the section is within a specified tolerance around the solution x.

\[ \qquad D(x) = (x - x_0)^2 + (x^2 + 5 - y_0)^2 \] \[ \begin{aligned} \text{phi} &\rightarrow \varphi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} = 1.618034 \\ \text{resphi} &\rightarrow \varphi_{res} = 2 - \varphi = 0.381966 \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= a + \varphi_{res}(b - a) & x_2 &= b - \varphi_{res}(b - a) \end{aligned} \]

For points \([(0,0);\ (-4,0);\ (-8,0);\ (2,0);\ (6,0)]\)

1. \((0, 0) \rightarrow D(x) = x^2 + (x^2 + 5)^2\)
\[ \text{Section } [a, b] = [-10, 10] \] \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= a + \varphi_{res}(b - a) & x_2 &= b - \varphi_{res}(b - a) \\ &= -10 + 0.381966(10 - (-10)) & &= 10 - 0.381966(10 - (-10)) \\ x_1 &= -2.36068 & &= 2.36068 \end{aligned} \]

i)

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{x_1} &= D(x_1) = (-2.36068)^2 + ((-2.36068)^2 + 5)^2 \\ D(x_1) &= 117.3571 \\ f_{x_2} &= D(x_2) = (2.36068)^2 + ((2.36068)^2 + 5)^2 \\ D(x_2) &= 117.3571 \end{aligned} \]

\(f_{x_1} = f_{x_2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} a &= x_1 = -2.36068 \qquad f_{x_1} = f_{x_2} = 117.3571 \\ x_1 &= x_2 = 2.36068 \\ x_2 &= 10 - 0.381966(10 - (-2.36068)) \\ x_2 &= 5.27864 \end{aligned} \]

ii)

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{x_2} &= D(x_2) = (5.27864)^2 + ((5.27864)^2 + 5)^2 \\ &= 1107.9095 \end{aligned} \]

\(f_{x_1} < f_{x_2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} b &= x_2 = 5.27864 \qquad f_{x_2} = f_{x_1} = 117.3571 \\ x_2 &= x_1 = 2.36068 \\ x_1 &= -2.36068 + 0.381966(5.27864 - (-2.36068)) \\ x_1 &= 0.55728 \end{aligned} \]
2. \((-4, 0) \rightarrow D(x) = (x + 4)^2 + (x^2 + 5)^2\)
\[ [a, b] = [-0.5, 0.5] \] \[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= -0.11803 & x_2 &= 0.11803 \end{aligned} \]

i)

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{x_1} &= D(x_1) = (-0.11803 + 4)^2 + ((-0.11803)^2 + 5)^2 \\ &= 40.2092 \\ f_{x_2} &= D(x_2) = (0.11803 + 4)^2 + ((0.11803)^2 + 5)^2 \\ &= 42.6977 \end{aligned} \]

\(f_{x_1} < f_{x_2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} b &= x_2 = 0.11803 \qquad f_{x_2} = f_{x_1} = 40.2092 \\ x_2 &= x_1 = -0.11803 \\ x_1 &= -0.5 + 0.381966(0.11803 - (-0.5)) \\ x_1 &= -0.26393 \end{aligned} \]

ii)

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{x_1} &= D(x_1) = (-0.26393 + 4)^2 + ((-0.26393)^2 + 5)^2 \\ &= 39.6597 \end{aligned} \]

\(f_{x_1} < f_{x_2}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} b &= x_2 = -0.11803 \qquad f_{x_2} = f_{x_1} = 39.6597 \\ x_2 &= x_1 = -0.26393 \\ x_1 &= -0.5 + 0.381966(-0.11803 + 0.5) \\ x_1 &= -0.35410 \end{aligned} \]

Least-Square Method

Line Fitting

\[ y^*(x) = c_0 + c_1 x \]

Ideally, \(y^*(x) = y(x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} c_0 + (0)c_1 &= 0.5 \\ c_0 + (1)c_1 &= 1.5 \\ c_0 + (2)c_1 &= 3.5 \\ c_0 + (3)c_1 &= 7.5 \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{Error } e(x) = y^*(x) - y(x) = c_0 + c_1 x - y(x) \]

Find \(c_0, c_1\) to minimize \(\rho(c_0, c_1) = \lVert e(x) \rVert_2^2\) (sum of square errors)

\[ \rho = (c_0 - 0.5)^2 + (c_0 + 2c_1 - 3.5)^2 + (c_0 + c_1 - 1.5)^2 + (c_0 + 3c_1 - 7.5)^2 \] \[ \Rightarrow \text{Minimum when } \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_0} = \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_1} = 0 \] \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_0} &= 2(c_0 - 0.5) + 2(c_0 + 2c_1 - 3.5) + 2(c_0 + c_1 - 1.5) + 2(c_0 + 3c_1 - 7.5) \\ 0 &= 4c_0 + 6c_1 - 13 \qquad \text{I} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_1} &= 4(c_0 + 2c_1 - 3.5) + 2(c_0 + c_1 - 1.5) + 6(c_0 + 3c_1 - 7.5) \\ 0 &= 6c_0 + 14c_1 - 31 \qquad \text{II} \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{I:} \quad c_0 = -1.5c_1 + 3.25 \]

Sub into II:

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 &= -9c_1 + 19.5 + 14c_1 - 31 \\ c_1 &= 2.3 \end{aligned} \]

Sub \(c_1\) into I:

\[ \begin{aligned} c_0 &= -1.5(2.3) + 3.25 \\ &= -0.2 \end{aligned} \] \[ \therefore\ y^*(x) = 2.3x - 0.2 \]

Parabola Fitting

\[ y^*(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 \]

Set \(y^*(x) = y(x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} c_0 + (0)c_1 + (0)^2 c_2 &= 0.5 \\ c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 &= 3.5 \\ c_0 + c_1 + c_2 &= 1.5 \\ c_0 + 3c_1 + 9c_2 &= 7.5 \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{Error } e(x) = y^*(x) - y(x) = c_0 + c_1 x + c_2 x^2 - y(x) \]

Find \(c_0, c_1, c_2\) to minimize \(\rho(c_0, c_1, c_2) = \lVert e(x) \rVert_2^2\) (sum of squared errors)

\[ \rho = (c_0 - 0.5)^2 + (c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 - 3.5)^2 + (c_0 + c_1 + c_2 - 1.5)^2 + (c_0 + 3c_1 + 9c_2 - 7.5)^2 \] \[ \Rightarrow \text{Minimum when } \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_0} = \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_1} = \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_2} = 0 \] \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_0} &= 2(c_0 - 0.5) + 2(c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 - 3.5) + 2(c_0 + c_1 + c_2 - 1.5) + 2(c_0 + 3c_1 + 9c_2 - 7.5) \\ 0 &= 4c_0 + 6c_1 + 14c_2 - 13 \qquad \text{I} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_1} &= 4(c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 - 3.5) + 2(c_0 + c_1 + c_2 - 1.5) + 6(c_0 + 3c_1 + 9c_2 - 7.5) \\ 0 &= 6c_0 + 14c_1 + 36c_2 - 31 \qquad \text{II} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial c_2} &= 8(c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 - 3.5) + 2(c_0 + c_1 + c_2 - 1.5) + 18(c_0 + 3c_1 + 9c_2 - 7.5) \\ 0 &= 14c_0 + 36c_1 + 98c_2 - 83 \qquad \text{III} \end{aligned} \]

Solve 3 equation system with calculator:

\[ c_0 = 0.55, \quad c_1 = 0.05, \quad c_2 = 0.75 \] \[ \therefore\ y^*(x) = 0.75x^2 + 0.05x + 0.55 \]

Newton-Raphson Multivariate

Line Fitting

Define the Error Functions:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_0(c_0, c_1) &= c_0 - 0.5 \\ f_1(c_0, c_1) &= c_0 + c_1 - 1.5 \\ f_2(c_0, c_1) &= c_0 + 2c_1 - 3.5 \\ f_3(c_0, c_1) &= c_0 + 3c_1 - 7.5 \end{aligned} \]

Define the Jacobian:

\[ J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{df_0}{dc_0} & \frac{df_0}{dc_1} \\ \frac{df_1}{dc_0} & \frac{df_1}{dc_1} \\ \frac{df_2}{dc_0} & \frac{df_2}{dc_1} \\ \frac{df_3}{dc_0} & \frac{df_3}{dc_1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Pseudo-inverse of J:

\[ J^{-1} = (J^T J)^{-1} J^T \] \[ J^T J = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ (J^T J)^{-1} = \frac{1}{(4)(14) - (6)(6)} \begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & -0.3 \\ -0.3 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ (J^T J)^{-1} J^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & -0.3 \\ -0.3 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & 0.4 & 0.1 & -0.2 \\ -0.3 & -0.1 & 0.1 & 0.3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Now,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_{n+1} = \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_n - J^{-1} f(c_0, c_1) \]

First guess: \(c_0 = 0,\ c_1 = 0\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & 0.4 & 0.1 & -0.2 \\ -0.3 & -0.1 & 0.1 & 0.3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ -1.5 \\ -3.5 \\ -7.5 \end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 \\ -2.3 \end{bmatrix} \\ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.2 \\ 2.3 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.2 \\ 2.3 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & 0.4 & 0.1 & -0.2 \\ -0.3 & -0.1 & 0.1 & 0.3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -0.7 \\ 0.6 \\ 0.9 \\ -0.8 \end{bmatrix} \\ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.2 \\ 2.3 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \\ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \end{bmatrix}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} -0.2 \\ 2.3 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{Converged} \end{aligned} \] \[ \therefore\ y^*(x) = 2.3x - 0.2 \]

Parabola Fitting

Define the Error Functions:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_0(c_0, c_1, c_2) &= c_0 - 0.5 \\ f_1(c_0, c_1, c_2) &= c_0 + c_1 + c_2 - 1.5 \\ f_2(c_0, c_1, c_2) &= c_0 + 2c_1 + 4c_2 - 3.5 \\ f_3(c_0, c_1, c_2) &= c_0 + 3c_1 + 9c_2 - 7.5 \end{aligned} \]

Define the Jacobian:

\[ J = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{df_0}{dc_0} & \frac{df_0}{dc_1} & \frac{df_0}{dc_2} \\ \frac{df_1}{dc_0} & \frac{df_1}{dc_1} & \frac{df_1}{dc_2} \\ \frac{df_2}{dc_0} & \frac{df_2}{dc_1} & \frac{df_2}{dc_2} \\ \frac{df_3}{dc_0} & \frac{df_3}{dc_1} & \frac{df_3}{dc_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \]

Pseudo-inverse of J:

\[ J^{-1} = (J^T J)^{-1} J^T \] \[ J^T J = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 & 14 \\ 6 & 14 & 36 \\ 14 & 36 & 98 \end{bmatrix} \] \[ \begin{aligned} (J^T J)^{-1} &= \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 4 & 6 & 14 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 6 & 14 & 36 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 14 & 36 & 98 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \\ &= \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 1.5 & 3.5 & 0.25 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 15 & -1.5 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 15 & 49 & -3.5 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \\ &= \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 1.5 & 3.5 & 0.25 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & -0.3 & 0.2 & 0 \\ 0 & 15 & 49 & -3.5 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \\ &= \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & -1 & 0.7 & -0.3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & -0.3 & 0.2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 & 1 & -3 & 1 \end{array}\right] \\ &= \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & -1 & 0.7 & -0.3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & -0.3 & 0.2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0.25 & -0.75 & 0.25 \end{array}\right] \\ &= \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0.95 & -1.05 & 0.25 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1.05 & 2.45 & -0.75 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0.25 & -0.75 & 0.25 \end{array}\right] \end{aligned} \] \[ (J^T J)^{-1} J^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 & -1.05 & 0.25 \\ -1.05 & 2.45 & -0.75 \\ 0.25 & -0.75 & 0.25 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 & 0.15 & -0.15 & 0.05 \\ -1.05 & 0.65 & 0.85 & -0.45 \\ 0.25 & -0.25 & -0.25 & 0.25 \end{bmatrix} \]

Now,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_{n+1} = \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_n - J^{-1} f(c_n) \]

Initial guess: \(c_0 = 0,\ c_1 = 0,\ c_2 = 0\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 & 0.15 & -0.15 & 0.05 \\ -1.05 & 0.65 & 0.85 & -0.45 \\ 0.25 & -0.25 & -0.25 & 0.25 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ -1.5 \\ -3.5 \\ -7.5 \end{bmatrix} \\ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -0.55 \\ -0.05 \\ -0.75 \end{bmatrix} \\ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.55 \\ 0.05 \\ 0.75 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.55 \\ 0.05 \\ 0.75 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.95 & 0.15 & -0.15 & 0.05 \\ -1.05 & 0.65 & 0.85 & -0.45 \\ 0.25 & -0.25 & -0.25 & 0.25 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.05 \\ -0.15 \\ 0.15 \\ -0.05 \end{bmatrix} \\ \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ c_2 \end{bmatrix}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0.55 \\ 0.05 \\ 0.75 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.55 \\ 0.05 \\ 0.75 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{Converged} \end{aligned} \] \[ \therefore\ y^*(x) = 0.75x^2 + 0.05x + 0.55 \]

Note that for both the line and parabola fitting, the exact solution is found after the first iteration. This is due to the nature of this problem: The objective functions are all linear combinations of the coefficients of a polynomial, So the Jacobian only consists of constants and doesn't depend on the iterations of the parameters. This is similar to trying to apply the normal Newton-Raphson to a line: the algorithm converges after one step. The initial guess doesn't matter, and will be further shown in the code below.
